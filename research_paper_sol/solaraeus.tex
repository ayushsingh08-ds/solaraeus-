\begin{abstract}
Decoupled urban microclimate simulations traditionally require complete recalculation of the spatial domain whenever localized shading interventions are introduced, creating a severe computational bottleneck for iterative design and optimization. We introduce Solaraeus, a 3D microclimate modeling platform that addresses this recomputation overhead through a mathematically certified incremental radiative transfer algorithm accelerated by resident CUDA GPU kernels. By bounding solid-angle decay and Stefan--Boltzmann linearization within a strict biometeorological tolerance ($\varepsilon_T = 0.50$~K), the engine reliably reuses over 98\% of grid cells. For the primary pedestrian corridor in Berlin Friedrichstra{\ss}e ($240\times 200$~m, 1.0~m resolution), GPU incremental updates execute in 18.0~ms, yielding a $39.4\times$ to $313.8\times$ speedup over full CPU recomputation. Autonomous spatial optimization identifies two-canopy configurations ($48.19~\mathrm{m}^2$) delivering up to 13.14~K of localized Mean Radiant Temperature ($\Tmrt$) cooling ($+3.33$~K UTCI relief), with a corridor-wide mean reduction of 0.48~K. Simulated cross-city replications reveal that while deep high-rise canyons exhibit limited intervention efficacy due to building self-shading (Chicago, $+0.12$~K peak cooling), wide pedestrian boulevards benefit substantially from targeted overhead shading (London, $+15.66$~K peak cooling).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Human-induced global warming, in combination with heat entrapment due to city architecture, results in extreme thermal discomfort for pedestrians in city centers during the severe summer heatwaves \cite{oke1982canyon,grimmond2010climate}. Daytime outdoor thermal sensation is mostly dependent on the Mean Radiant Temperature ($\Tmrt$) rather than dry-bulb air temperature alone \cite{matzarakis2007modelling}. Due to short-wave direct-beam solar radiation, diffuse solar radiation, and long-wave radiation from heated streets and building facades, the physiological response of pedestrians to the heat within sunny city street canyons becomes a Strong and Very Strong Heat Stress according to Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) \cite{jendritzky2012universal,blazejczyk2013introduction}. Thus, the urban planners of cities need accurate microclimatic models to assess the effectiveness of local heat reduction strategies like shading facilities and trees on boulevards \cite{krayenhoff2021cooling}.

Existing urban microclimate modeling approaches are divided between the microscale Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) with decoupled radiation models. Micro-scale CFD simulations like ENVI-met \cite{bruse1998simulating} capture atmospheric turbulence and vegetation heat fluxes, but take hours to days to compute one day of data, making iterative design difficult. On the other hand, decoupled radiation models such as SOLWEIG in the Urban Multi-scale Environmental Predictor (UMEP) \cite{lindberg2008solweig,lindberg2018urban} decouple the calculation of geometric radiation transfer from fluid dynamics calculations. Using ray marching on digital surface models to calculate sky view factors (SVF) and direct shadowing, decoupled radiative transfer engines reduce runtime from days to seconds while maintaining the physiological accuracy in radiant heat flux calculations \cite{gal2009sky,do2024geospatial}.

Even with these improvements, there remain three interrelated drawbacks to current simulation frameworks. Firstly, present-day solvers function as single large-scale batch programs; each time a local architectural change is made, such as adding an awning or canopy, the engine resets the domain and computes all grid cell values from scratch. Secondly, existing heuristic spatial clipping techniques fail to provide a mathematically proven closed-form error bound that guarantees that the receptor points which remain unchanged are within biometeorologically acceptable tolerance levels ($|\widetilde{T}_{\mathrm{mrt}}(x) - T_{\mathrm{mrt}}^{\mathrm{full}}(x)| \le \varepsilon_T$). Thirdly, planners utilize manual trial-and-error techniques due to the multi-parameter continuous search space of canopy dimensions, orientations, heights, and no overlap requirements.

To overcome these obstacles, this project aims at achieving two main goals:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Design, demonstrate, and validate an accredited incremental radiative transfer model that assures thermal precision below one degree ($\varepsilon_T = 0.50$~K) through closed-form Stefan--Boltzmann linearization and solid-angle attenuation together with an integrated CUDA GPU engine for sub-20~ms latency updates.
    \item Evaluate the cooling efficiency and optimization features of localized shades in international urban pathways by means of four different optimization techniques (Random Search, Genetic Algorithms, Static Gaussian Process Approximations, and Active Learning) through identical simulation budgets on digital twin models of Berlin (main), Chicago (replication), and London (replication).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Radiation Modeling and Decoupled Microclimate Engines}
Urban microclimates have historically been simulated using coupled formulations like ENVI-met \cite{bruse1998simulating} and PALM-4U \cite{eingruber2025simulation}. While physically comprehensive, full Navier--Stokes fluid simulations require hours on multi-core clusters per diurnal cycle, making iterative design impractical \cite{krayenhoff2021cooling}. Decoupled radiation models like SOLWEIG \cite{lindberg2008solweig,lindberg2018urban} resolve this by computing shortwave and longwave fluxes separately from airflow, evaluating pedestrian thermal comfort in seconds. Recent studies have extended decoupled modeling to solar shading \cite{rayegan2025development}, urban canyons \cite{bourquin2025improved}, retroreflective pavements \cite{yuan2025harnessing}, and vegetative cooling \cite{baia2026scalable,chen2024investigating}. However, existing engines recompute the entire domain from scratch whenever a local intervention (such as a fabric canopy or tree) is introduced.

\subsection{GPU Acceleration and Precomputed Transport Models}
To speed up big simulations, recent approaches have made use of GPU parallelization. Kamath et~al. \cite{kamath2026solweig} created SOLWEIG-GPU for comfort simulation on the city scale through CUDA ray marching, whereas Mei et~al. \cite{mei2025gust1} created GUST1.0 for surface energy balance calculations with GPU, and Nguyen et~al. \cite{nguyen2023mathematical} employed geospatial parallel algorithms. In computer graphics, Freude et~al. \cite{freude2025inverse,freude2023precomputed} utilized pre-computed transport matrices of radiative transport in order to simulate dynamic thermal exchange in real-time. Nevertheless, pre-computed matrices assume static geometry; by adding even just one canopy, the whole model becomes outdated. Hence, existing GPU approaches accelerate full recalculation, but do not utilize unaffected cells incrementally.

\subsection{Machine Learning Surrogates and Active Optimization}
Surrogate modeling has also emerged to bypass heavy physics simulations \cite{do2024geospatial,tao2025integrating}. For example, Qin et~al. \cite{qin2024modeling} used localized Fourier Neural Operators to predict 3D microclimate fields in milliseconds. Yet neural surrogates lack certified physical error bounds and struggle with sharp shadow boundaries in complex canyons \cite{tao2025integrating}. While Bayesian active learning balances exploration and exploitation, it still requires an underlying physics engine that can evaluate candidate designs in sub-second time with verified numerical accuracy.

\subsection{Research Gap}
In summary, current decoupled physics engines offer validated biometeorological accuracy but suffer from monolithic recalculation overhead. Conversely, precomputed transport matrices and neural surrogates offer real-time speeds but break under geometric modifications or lack physical error guarantees. A framework that unites mathematically certified incremental radiative transfer with resident GPU acceleration and active canopy optimization has been missing. Solaraeus directly fills this gap.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Complete Simulation and Optimization Pipeline}
Figure~\ref{fig:methodology} shows the entire end-to-end workflow of Solaraeus. The framework consists of six interdependent phases: (1) Data input, taking in building CityGML models, DTM grids, tree inventories, and hourly weather CSVs; (2) Preprocessing, involving the alignment of coordinate reference systems (EPSG:25833, 32616, 27700), creation of a 1.0~m pedestrian assessment grid at 1.1~m eye height, and setting of material albedo ($\alpha = 0.15\text{--}0.30$) and emissivity ($\varepsilon = 0.90\text{--}0.95$); (3) Physical model core, comprising of solar position astronomical calculation, double precision M{\"o}ller--Trumbore ray-triangle direct shading algorithm, Steyn (1980) 36-azimuth radial Sky View Factor computation, six-flux cylindrical heat exchange, and Stefan--Boltzmann $\Tmrt$ inversion; (4) Computational speedup, offering a CPU FP64 solver reference and a resident CUDA GPU kernel with verified incremental state caching functionality; (5) Active learning surrogates, and recommending interventions through lower confidence bound acquisitions; and (6) Post-processing, rendering the spatial difference maps between $\Tmrt$ and $\UTCI$.

\subsection{Biometeorological Radiation Engine and Corridor Assessment Domain}
The microclimatic physics engine evaluates directional radiant fluxes onto a standardized standing human cylinder ($1.1$~m eye-level receptor height) according to six-flux biometeorological principles \cite{lindberg2008solweig,matzarakis2007modelling}. Direct solar beam irradiance incident on pedestrian receptor $x$ is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
K_{\mathrm{dir}}(x) = I_{\mathrm{dir}} \left[ f_{\mathrm{up}} \sin\gamma_s + f_{\mathrm{side}} \sum_{d \in \{\pm x, \pm y\}} \max(0, \hat{\mathbf{s}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}_d) \right] M_{\mathrm{sh}}(x)
\end{equation}
where $I_{\mathrm{dir}}$ is direct normal irradiance ($\Wm$), $\gamma_s$ is the solar altitude angle, $\hat{\mathbf{s}} = (s_x, s_y, s_z)$ is the normalized solar position unit vector, $\hat{\mathbf{n}}_d$ are cardinal unit normal vectors, $M_{\mathrm{sh}}(x) \in \{0, 1\}$ is the binary shadow mask evaluated via double-precision M{\"o}ller--Trumbore ray-triangle intersection, and human body projection weighting factors are $f_{\mathrm{up}} = f_{\mathrm{down}} = 0.06$ and $f_{\mathrm{side}} = 0.22$.

Sky View Factor (SVF) $\Psi(x)$ is computed via the Steyn (1980) 36-azimuth discrete radial horizon integration:
\begin{equation}
\Psi(x) = \frac{1}{N_{\phi}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\phi}} \cos^2\bigl(\beta(\phi_i)\bigr) = \frac{1}{N_{\phi}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\phi}} \frac{1}{1 + \tan^2\bigl(\beta(\phi_i)\bigr)}
\end{equation}
where $N_{\phi} = 36$ discrete azimuth angles $\phi_i = 2\pi(i-1)/N_{\phi}$, $\beta(\phi_i) = \arctan\left(\max_{d \le d_{\max}} \frac{\Delta h(d)}{d}\right)$ represents the maximum horizon obstacle elevation angle along azimuth $\phi_i$, and $d_{\max} = 60.0$~m is the horizon search radius.

Diffuse sky and surface-reflected shortwave fluxes are integrated as:
\begin{align}
K_{\mathrm{diff}}(x) &= f_{\mathrm{up}} D_{\mathrm{diff}} \Psi(x) + f_{\mathrm{down}} \alpha_g \left[I_{\mathrm{dir}}\sin\gamma_s M_{\mathrm{sh}}(x) + D_{\mathrm{diff}}\Psi(x)\right] \nonumber \\
&\quad + 4 f_{\mathrm{side}} \left[ 0.5 D_{\mathrm{diff}}\Psi(x) + 0.5 \alpha_w (1 - \Psi(x))(I_{\mathrm{dir}} + D_{\mathrm{diff}}) \right]
\end{align}
yielding total incident shortwave radiation $K(x) = K_{\mathrm{dir}}(x) + K_{\mathrm{diff}}(x)$, where $\alpha_g$ and $\alpha_w$ are ground and building wall surface albedos.

Six-directional longwave thermal radiation accounts for sky, wall, and ground emissions:
\begin{align}
L_{\mathrm{up}}(x) &= \Psi(x) \varepsilon_{\mathrm{air}} \sigma T_a^4 + (1 - \Psi(x)) \varepsilon_w \sigma T_w^4 \\
L_{\mathrm{down}}(x) &= \varepsilon_g \sigma T_g^4 \\
L_{\mathrm{side}}(x) &= 0.5 \Psi(x) \varepsilon_{\mathrm{air}} \sigma T_a^4 + (1 - 0.5 \Psi(x)) \varepsilon_w \sigma T_w^4
\end{align}
where clear-sky atmospheric emissivity $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{air}}$ is determined by the Prata (1996) precipitable water formulation, $\sigma = 5.670374 \times 10^{-8}~\WmK$ is the Stefan--Boltzmann constant, and $T_a, T_w, T_g$ are air, wall, and ground absolute temperatures in Kelvin. Total incident longwave radiation is $L_{\mathrm{total}}(x) = f_{\mathrm{up}} L_{\mathrm{up}}(x) + f_{\mathrm{down}} L_{\mathrm{down}}(x) + 4 f_{\mathrm{side}} L_{\mathrm{side}}(x)$.

The total absorbed radiant flux density $S_{\mathrm{str}}(x)$ and Mean Radiant Temperature $\Tmrt(x)$ are derived through Stefan--Boltzmann inversion:
\begin{equation}
S_{\mathrm{str}}(x) = a_k K(x) + a_l L_{\mathrm{total}}(x)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{mrt}}(x) = \left( \frac{S_{\mathrm{str}}(x)}{a_l \sigma} \right)^{1/4} - 273.15
\end{equation}
where $a_k = 0.70$ is the standard human clothing/skin shortwave absorption coefficient and $a_l = 0.97$ is the longwave emissivity coefficient \cite{lindberg2008solweig}.

\textbf{Corridor-Mean Assessment Domain Definition:} The primary urban evaluation domain $\Omega_{\mathrm{corridor}}$ represents the central pedestrian walkway of the street canyon, formally defined in local Euclidean coordinates as:
\begin{equation}
\Omega_{\mathrm{corridor}} = \left\{ (x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \;\middle|\; -10.0\,\mathrm{m} \le x \le 10.0\,\mathrm{m}, \; -40.0\,\mathrm{m} \le y \le 40.0\,\mathrm{m} \right\}
\end{equation}
at pedestrian assessment height $z_{\mathrm{ped}} = 1.1$~m. At the uniform $1.0$~m simulation grid resolution, $\Omega_{\mathrm{corridor}}$ comprises exactly 1,600 contiguous receptor cells ($20~\mathrm{m} \times 80~\mathrm{m} = 1,600~\mathrm{m}^2$). All reported corridor-mean biometeorological objective metrics (Mean $\Tmrt$, Mean $\UTCI$, $P_{90}$, and thermal cooling deltas) are formally evaluated by spatial averaging across this designated domain $\Omega_{\mathrm{corridor}}$.

\subsection{Certified Incremental Radiative Transfer Formulation}
Figure~\ref{fig:flowchart} illustrates the flow chart of the certified incremental algorithm, specifying the mathematically proven elements from those that are calibrated empirically. On introducing a localized canopy perturbation $\Delta\mathcal{M}$, the solver isolates a conservative set of impacted pixels $\mathcal{A}$. By Theorem~1, for every receptor pixel $x \notin \mathcal{A}$, the Mean Radiant Temperature approximation error is bounded by:
\begin{equation}
|\widetilde{T}_{\mathrm{mrt}}(x) - T_{\mathrm{mrt}}^{\mathrm{full}}(x)| \le B_T(x) \le \varepsilon_T = 0.50\,\mathrm{K}
\end{equation}
This inequality is derived using three physical components: (1) bounds on the direct beam guaranteeing no change to the direct shadows outside the dilated projection cone; (2) solid angle horizon truncation demonstrating quadratic decrease of the sky view factor perturbation with respect to distance $d$, $\Delta\Psi \le (H_{\mathrm{canopy}} \cdot W_{\mathrm{canopy}})/(2\pi d^2)$; and (3) concavity of the Stefan--Boltzmann law limiting temperature perturbations by $\Delta\Tmrt \le \Delta\Phi/(4\varepsilon_p\sigma(T_{\min})^3)$. In case of sun altitude lower than $5^\circ$ or bound larger than $\varepsilon_T$, the engine reverts to full-domain recomputation, guaranteeing absence of certificate violations.

\subsection{Multiple Optimization Techniques and Active Learning Loop}
Figure~\ref{fig:opt_loop} depicts the optimization loop for autonomous placement of the canopy. The optimization problem aims at finding continuously-valued coordinates, sizes, orientation, and height for $K = 2$ canopies (decision dimensions $D = 12$) which minimize pedestrian pathway mean $\Tmrt$ such that: (1) total area $\le 50.0~\mathrm{m}^2$; (2) no pairwise overlap; (3) setback from building walls $\ge 1.0$~m; and (4) clearance under the canopies $\ge 3.0$~m. Four approaches have been compared under identical conditions in terms of budget ($N = 50$ physics evaluations) and seeds: Uniform Random Search, Direct Genetic Algorithm (GA), static Gaussian Process Surrogate model, and Active Learning through Lower Confidence Bound (GP-LCB) acquisition: $\alpha_{\mathrm{LCB}}(\theta) = \mu(\theta) - \kappa\sigma(\theta)$ with $\kappa = 1.96$. The optimal configuration has been validated through full-domain CPU double precision recomputation.

\subsection{Physical Accuracy Validation and Independent Parity Checks}
Physical accuracy was verified by comparing to the reference SOLWEIG/UMEP model for a well-defined $60 \times 60$~m plot in the Berlin corridor (900 street receptor cells). The correlation between reference SOLWEIG outputs and Solaraeus is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:physics_val}. The absorbed cylindrical irradiance agreed almost perfectly ($\mathrm{MAE} = 3.07~\Wm$, $\mathrm{RMSE} = 3.09~\Wm$, $R^2 = 0.9922$). $\Tmrt$ accuracy was checked as follows: $\mathrm{MAE} = 0.437$~K, $\mathrm{RMSE} = 0.439$~K, maximum error $= 0.613$~K. Physical limit validation distinguished between closed-form analytic benchmarks and physical consistency/determinism sanity checks: (a) isolated cube direct flux was analytically verified against exact closed-form trigonometric projections (error $= 0.00~\Wm$); (b) blackbody isothermal enclosure converged to the analytical boundary temperature (error $= 0.0000$~K); (c) overcast sky demonstrated physically consistent diffuse attenuation (reducing corridor-mean $\Tmrt$ by $10.01$~K); (d) ground albedo parametric sweep exhibited strictly monotonic radiant flux increase ($+39.5~\Wm$ across $\alpha = 0.10\text{--}0.50$); and (e) optimizer-off repeated runs verified bitwise deterministic numerical reproducibility.

\subsection{Computational Scalability and GPU Acceleration Performance Metrics}

The GPU backend executes resident CUDA ray-tracing kernels via CuPy memory pools without host-device synchronization during iterations.

Sensitivity analysis under seven environmental scenarios revealed three key parameters that caused changes in thermal indicators exceeding 1.0~K: (1) Elimination of mature roadside trees resulted in $+1.3812$~K of corridor mean $\Tmrt$, highlighting the importance of the role of urban vegetation; (2) Changing wind speed by $\pm 1.0$~m/s shifted peak $\UTCI$ by $-0.97$~K (for $+1.0$~m/s increased convection) and $+0.81$~K (for $-1.0$~m/s reduced convection) under continuous 6th-order polynomial evaluation (yielding $\mp 1.0$~K under conventional $0.1^\circ\mathrm{C}$ discretisation); and (3) Changing direct beam irradiance by $\pm 10\%$ yielded $\pm 3.76$~K change of peak $\Tmrt$. Changes in building heights by $\pm 5\%$ resulted in marginal changes in thermal indicators ranging from $-0.06$~K to $+0.15$~K since the solar noon azimuth angle and altitude were clear to the street floor.
Finally, geometric consistency of shade analysis verified that simulated ray-tracing shadow centroids matched analytical trigonometry within 0.095~m (Canopy 1) and 0.006~m (Canopy 2), well within the 1.0~m grid cell resolution.

\section{Discussion}

The findings suggest that the localized shade structures are effective at alleviating local heat stress ($+13.14$~K to $+15.66$~K $\Tmrt$ decrease; $+3.33$~K to $+3.82$~K $\UTCI$ improvement) despite causing mild corridor-wide cooling ($+0.40$~K to $+0.48$~K). The findings support previous research by Eingr{\"u}ber et al. (2025) \cite{eingruber2025simulation} employing PALM-4U in Berlin that showed the localized vegetative cooling range from 12.0~K to 15.0~K under dense tree canopies. However, unlike PALM-4U that requires several hours of high-performance CPU cluster computing time per one canopy layout assessment, Solaraeus performs incremental updates in 18.0~ms on a laptop GPU. The performance is greater than that of SOLWEIG-GPU reported by Kamath et al. (2026) \cite{kamath2026solweig} as a result of GPU ray tracing enhanced with certified mathematical spatial caching.

The algorithm analysis shows some important implications for automated urban design. While active learning and Bayesian optimization are widely recognized as best practices for engineering design \cite{tao2025integrating,qin2024modeling}, Active Learning failed to outperform the simple Random Search or Genetic Algorithms statistically in strict budgets (Welch’s t-test $p = 0.514$). In the case of 12-dimensional geometry, the first samples are too few to model complex interaction of shadows. Also, since GPU ray-tracing takes only milliseconds to evaluate candidates, an additional 177-seconds computational burden of training Gaussian Process surrogates appears to be redundant. As mentioned by Chen et al. (2024) \cite{chen2024investigating}, the local authorities tend to focus on rapid candidate screening over heavy surrogate training in early-stage design.

\subsection{Boundaries and Limitations of the Science}
Some physical limitations should be mentioned. Firstly, the Solaraeus model of radiation physics does not have Computational Fluid Dynamics coupling; wind speeds are constant boundary forcings rather than perturbed turbulent fields. Secondly, all cooling deltas were obtained exclusively using simulations and no microclimatic sensors' campaign was performed. Thirdly, crowns of trees are modeled as porous geometrical shapes of cylinders and ellipsoids with transmissivity coefficient ($\tau = 0.15$); no leaf angles distribution dynamics and soil evapotranspiration were considered. Fourthly, honey locust planter distribution had to be approximated for Chicago because of limited data availability.

% =========================================================================
\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

The Solaraeus architecture addresses the bottleneck for microclimatic recomputation by using incremental and verified radiative transfer models along with GPU acceleration. With a proven Stefan--Boltzmann bound for errors (0.50~K), the platform is able to reuse more than 98\% of static spatial cells, attaining update latencies below 20~ms even on consumer-class computers. Multiple simulated replications across various cities have revealed that installing shades in targeted areas offers substantial improvement in terms of comfort in wide boulevards (London, $+15.66$~K maximum cooling), but has negligible effect in self-shadowed high-rise canyons (Chicago, $+0.12$~K maximum cooling). For continuous canopy design, direct evolutionary algorithms were faster and more stable compared to Gaussian Process based active learning algorithms.

In future, the framework would be expanded in three ways: (1) coupling of resident GPU radiation models with fast kinematic wind solvers to address canyon turbulence issues; (2) spectral albedo and retro-reflective pavement coatings \cite{yuan2025harnessing}; and (3) implementation of digital twin framework on tropical Asian urban environments like the Bangalore and other metropolitan corridors of India to study monsoon and pre-monsoon heatwaves dynamics.
